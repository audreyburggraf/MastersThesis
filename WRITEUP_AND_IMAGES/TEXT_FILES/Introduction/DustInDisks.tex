

A protostellar disk is composed mostly of gas (99\%), with the remaining 1\% being dust. The dust provides the raw material that eventually forms terrestrial planets and the cores of giant planets \citep{Williams, Birnstiel2016}. The dust in protostellar disks originates from the ISM and is typically sub-micron in size \citep{Williams}.



\subsection{Dust Evolution and Grain Growth \status{ready for tweak}}
Although dust only accounts for 1\% of the disk's initial mass, it plays a vital role in the evolution of the disk and the formation of planets \citep{Williams}. Dust grain growth, or dust coagulation, is an early stage of planet formation in which grain-grain collisions can cause dust particles to stick together and form larger particles. During this phase, the grains begin as (sub-?)micron-sized particles and can grow, eventually leading to the formation of planetesimals  \citep{Testi}. 


During a grain-grain collision, the grains do not always stick together to become larger. The grains can also bounce off each other, leaving their sizes mostly unchanged, or fragment and produce smaller grains, either with or without mass transfer. The result of the collision depends on the velocity and relative sizes of the particles colliding \citep{Testi}.



\subsection{Dust Distribution and Disk Structure \status{ready for tweak}}

As grains change in size, their interactions with the surrounding gas also change, altering how they are transported both vertically and radially within the disk \cite{Andrews}. 

 



The solid-body dust grains orbit faster than the gas, as they do not feel gas pressure, and experience a drag force \citep{Krumholz}. The drag force decreases the angular momentum of the grain, causing it to drift radially inward toward the centre of the disk \citep{Krumholz}. 


Additionally, dust grains tend to vertically settle onto the midplane of the disk. This result greatly depends on the grain size. As the grain size increases, its surface-to-mass ratio decreases, and the motion decouples from the gas, also creating a downward drag force \citep{Williams}. 



As these collisions, gravitational effects and turbulent mixing all work together, we are left with larger grains residing on the midplane, with smaller grains higher up. The disk is flared (larger scale height further from the centre) as a result of the fact that the farther from the star, the weaker its gravitational influence. \subsection{Effects of Grain Size on Observed Emission \status{ready for tweaking}}
\label{sec: intro_dust_grainsize}

Dust grains dominate the opacity in the disk, controlling how radiation is absorbed, scattered and emitted \citep{Williams}. The opacity depends on several properties of the dust, including its shape, composition and size \citep{Kataoka_2015}. 

% Different grain sizes absorb radiation differently
At millimetre wavelengths, the continuum emission from disks is dominated by thermal emission from the dust, which is controlled by the opacity of the dust \citep{Testi}. The efficiency with which the dust grains absorb and emit radiation depends on their size relative to the observing wavelength  \citep{Testi}. 

% Different grain sizes scatter radiation differently 
Grain size also affects the scattering properties of dust. Smaller grain sizes at millimetre wavelengths are less efficient at scattering light \citep{Kataoka_2015}. However, larger grain sizes that are close to the observing wavelength are much more efficient at scattering light \citep{Kataoka_2015}.

Because the absorption and scattering properties of dust grains depend on their size relative to the observing wavelength, observations at millimetre wavelengths can be used to study dust grain growth in disks \citep{Testi}. 




Additionally, dust self-scattering is most efficient when dust grain sizes are comparable with the wavelength:
\begin{equation}
  a_{max} = \lambda / 2 \pi
  \label{eq: scattering_efficiency}
\end{equation}
\noindent \citep{Stephens_2017} (see size parameter, Equation \ref{eq: size_parameter}). Therefore, scattering depends on the observing wavelength, and each wavelength naturally probes a different dust grain size. Each wavelength probes a different grain size, so the more wavelengths of data you have, the more grain sizes you probe, and the better constraint you can place on the grain size.

\subsection{Polarization and Stokes Parameters \status{ready to tweak}}


Polarized light is light where the electric field has a preferred direction. For linearly polarized light, the electric field oscillates along a fixed direction perpendicular to the direction in which the light propagates \citep{RybickiLightman} (see Figure \ref{fig: LinearPol}). Polarization information from interferometers (such as ALMA) is represented by the Stokes visibilities from cross-correlations between antennas in the array \citep{HullDebias}. The Stokes parameter $I$ is the total intensity of the wave of light. \SQ measures the difference in intensity between the North-South and East-West components. \SU measures the difference between the components at 45$\circ$ and 135$\circ$ relative to the North-South axis. This coordinate system can be seen in Figure \ref{fig: LinearPol}. Using $Q$ and $U$ together reveals information about the strength and orientation of the polarization (polarized intensity Equation \ref{eq: POLI}; polarization angle Equation \ref{eq: PA}). It is possible for there to be circular polarization, and this would introduce the Stokes $V$ term, but ALMA is not well calibrated for it, so we cannot observe it. \question{cite something?}

The polarized emission from disks, specifically their polarization angle to make vectors (Chapter \ref{sec: chap4_polarization_vectors}), reveals the polarization morphology, or pattern. The emission can be polarized from many different mechanisms in the disk, each leaving behind a different pattern, and each can help constrain different physical properties of the disk \citep{Stephens_2019}. Since the polarized emission at these wavelengths can be caused by different mechanisms, it is a challenge to disentangle them and determine what is causing it/interpret the polarization morphology \citep{Stephens_2017}, and as a result the mechanisms are unknown \citep{Kataoka_2017}. The following subsections explore a few of the potential patterns and mechanisms that cause them, but this is not a complete list. A visual example can be seen in Figure \ref{fig: kataoka_polarization_morphologies}. 



Polarization observations of polarized emission from dust grains in disks at (sub)millimetre wavelengths provide insight into dust grain growth in protostellar disks \citep{Harrison2024}.

\subsubsection{Magnetic Grain Alignment \status{ready to tweak}}



Magnetic grain alignment is produced when non-spherical dust grains align to a magnetic field morphology, causing the short axis of the dust grains to align parallel to the magnetic field direction via radiative torques  \citep{Stephens_2019, Harrison2024}. Typically in a disk, it would be toroidal alignment \citep{Stephens_2017}, which can be seen in Figure \ref{fig: kataoka_polarization_morphologies}(a). Polarized dust emission is known to trace the direction of the magnetic fields in the interstellar medium (ISM) and the molecular clouds in the outer part of the disks \citep{Stephens_2019}. Even though this pattern is what is expected, this is not the pattern that is seen in most disks; instead, it is better explained by other mechanisms (producing other patterns) \cite{Harrison2024}.

\subsubsection{Radiative Grain Alignment \status{ready to tweak}}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Another polarization mechanism is caused by the long axis of the grains aligning perpendicular to the radiation field (k-RAT alignment) \citep{Stephens_2017}. This produces a polarization morphology that is azimuthal, or in a circular pattern about the centre of the disk, as seen in Figure \ref{fig: kataoka_polarization_morphologies}(b). However, there are other polarization mechanisms that can produce an azimuthal polarization morphology other than radiative alignment. This includes mechanical alignment, where grains align with their long axis in the streaming direction \citep{Stephens_2019}, aerodynamic alignment where gas and dust orbit the central star at different speeds, or other mechanisms. It is unknown exactly what causes this azimuthal morphology, and determining this is beyond the scope of this project. For this project, we just need to know that a common morphological pattern that is seen is this azimuthal one, and by default it may be written that it's caused by aligning with the radiation field, but it could be from other methods.

\subsubsection{Dust Self-Scattering \status{ ready for tweak}}
\label{sec: dust_self_scattering}



Dust self-scattering is when thermal emission from dust grains is scattered by other dust grains in the disk, producing polarized light. This polarization mechanism is expected to produce a polarization morphology that is parallel to the disk minor axis \citep{Kataoka_2017}, as shown in Figure \ref{fig: kataoka_polarization_morphologies}(c). The efficiency of dust self-scattering polarization is highly dependent on the maximum grain size (see Chapter \ref{sec: c5_P_and_w_vs_a}), making it the ideal mechanism to focus on in this project, as it is a good constraint for the maximum dust grain size \citep{Harrison2024}.

\subsection{Why (Sub)millimetre Observations? \status{More words? Needs tweaking}}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
Most of a protostellar disk is cool enough ($<$100 K) such that it efficiently emits thermal (continuum)  radiation at (sub)millimetre wavelengths \citep{Andrews}. At these wavelengths, disks are normally optically thin, therefore, the emission probes the cool disk interior where most of the mass in the disk is, and we can see deeper into the disk \cite{Birnstiel2016}.

\subsection{ALMA \status{ready to tweak}}
The Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array
(ALMA) is an international radio telescope located in the Atacama Desert in northern Chile \citep{Wotten}. It has 66 antennas working together as an interferometer, with multiple array configurations and baselines ranging from 15 meters to approximately 15 km \citep{Wotten}. ALMA has an observing wavelength range of 0.3 to 10 mm \citep{Wotten}, allowing it to probe thermal emission from dust in protostellar disks. The long baselines between antennas provide high angular resolution and sensitivity, enabling the study of structure in disks and their polarization morphology to be spatially resolved \citep{Testi}.

\subsection{Multi-wavelength Observations \status{ready to tweak}}
\label{sec: intro_mw}
% \note{In the plot of Pw vs a max I refer to this for the importance of mw observations for grain size}



As mentioned in Chapter \ref{sec: intro_polarization_mechanisms}, the observed polarization morphology and inferred dominant polarization mechanism have been found to change with observing wavelength. However, the dominant polarization mechanism does not depend on wavelength alone. Different observing wavelengths are sensitive to different grain sizes and optical depths \citep{Testi}, which reveal different mechanisms. By comparing the polarization fraction and morphology at multiple wavelengths, it is possible to disentangle the polarization mechanisms and place stronger constraints on grain sizes compared to what could be achieved with observations at a single wavelength \citep{Harrison2024, Kataoka_2015}. 



Additionally, dust self-scattering is most efficient when dust grain sizes are comparable with the wavelength:
\begin{equation}
  a_{max} = \lambda / 2 \pi
  \label{eq: scattering_efficiency}
\end{equation}
\noindent \citep{Stephens_2017} (see size parameter, Equation \ref{eq: size_parameter}). Therefore, scattering depends on the observing wavelength, and each wavelength naturally probes a different dust grain size. Each wavelength probes a different grain size, so the more wavelengths of data you have, the more grain sizes you probe, and the better constraint you can place on the grain size.

\subsection{Observational Example: HL Tau \status{ Ready for tweak}}
\label{sec: intro_HL_Tau}

HL Tau is one of the brightest Class I/II disks at (sub)mm wavelengths \citep{Stephens_2017}. It has been the focus of many studies using ALMA polarization observations to study its morphology, including observations at 3.1 mm in \cite{Kataoka_2017}, \bsix in \cite{Stephens_2017}, \bseven originally presented in \cite{Stephens_2017} with higher-resolution data in \cite{Stephens2023}. More recently, in \cite{Lin2024}, data at \bfour and \bfive were presented, combining previous observations. We will look at \bfourNS, \bfiveNS, \bsixNS and \bseven, as those are the wavelengths used in this project. 


With these wavelengths, the polarization morphologies are presented in Figure \ref{fig: HLTauLin2024}. This figure shows that the polarization morphology changes with wavelength, indicating that the dominant polarization mechanism changes with wavelength. At higher wavelengths, the morphology is azimuthal, and as wavelength decreases, the vectors become more aligned with the minor axis of the disk. This highlights the importance of multiwavelength data, as observing HL Tau at one wavelength would only show one morphology, rather than showing the change with wavelength.

\subsection{Image Reconstruction}

The inverse Fourier transform of the complex visibilities in the \uv plane (Equation \ref{eq: V(u,v)}), given by the equation:
\begin{equation}
    I(l,m) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty V(u,v) e^{2\pi i(ul+vm)}dudv,
    \label{eq: I(l, m)}
\end{equation}
\noindent converts the visibilities into the image domain \citep{Thompson}. Since the \uv coverage is incomplete, this produces a `dirty image', rather than a perfect reconstruction \citep{Marvil2026}. Because of the incomplete \uv coverage, the dirty image is inherently convolved with a point spread function called the `dirty beam' \citep{Marvil2026}

\subsubsection{Weighting Schemes}
% ------------------------------------------------
Before the inverse Fourier transform is taken, each visibility in the \uv plane is given a weight, which influences the properties of the dirty beam \citep{Marvil2026}. When each visibility is weighted equally, this is called \emph{natural weighting}, which gives the best signal-to-noise ratio (\SNRns) for detecting faint objects \citep{Monnier, Thompson}. \SCC{When visibilities are weighted such that each portion of the \uv plane is weighted equally, it is called \emph{uniform weighting}, and gives a higher angular resolution but lower sensitivity} \citep{Monnier}. 

A `robust' parameter was introduced in \cite{briggs} for a weighting scheme called Briggs weighting. The robust parameter controls a balance between the two extremes of natural and uniform to find an ideal trade-off between \SNR and high angular resolution \citep{Marvil2026, Monnier}. The robust parameter goes from -2 to 2, where -2 is uniform weighting, 0 is midway, and 2 is natural weighting \citep{Thompson}

\subsection{Deconvolution (\clean)}
% ------------------------------------------------


Deconvolution is the process of removing the effects of the dirty beam from the dirty image, typically using the algorithm \clean \citep{Marvil2026, Thompson}. The first step in the \clean algorithm is to locate the brightest point of the dirty image, subtract a scaled copy of the dirty beam centred on this point, and record the position and brightness as \clean components \citep{Monnier}. This process is repeated iteratively until only the residual noise is left \citep{Thompson}. The image is then convolved with the final \clean beam, called the synthesized beam, which is a smooth Gaussian with the same full width at half maximum (FWHM) as the dirty beam \citep{Thompson}. The residual image is added back, producing the final \clean image \citep{Thompson}. 


\SC{It may be worth noting that clean builds a model of the image based on delta functions or small Gaussians of flux through an iterative process.}

\subsection{Self-Calibration}

Self-calibration is an additional calibration step that can be done after standard calibration.
In standard calibration sources such as quasars are used, but in self-calibration the science target itself is used as the calibrator for both phase and amplitude calibration \citep{Marvil2}. The process of self-calibration is useful to remove artifacts \SCC{from the source due to calibration errors, or remove artifacts from a background source due to direction-independent calibration errors} \citep{Marvil2}. The specific details on the self-calibration process used in this project are described in Chapter \ref{ch_data}.

To understand how planets form, we must study how dust grains in protostellar disks grow from micron-sized dust grains into larger objects. Part of understanding this process is determining how large the dust grains in a disk are relative to the expected grain sizes in the disk. Constraining the dust grains in a disk is a challenge as grains are small, far away, and cannot be measured directly. Polarized light from dust self-scattering provides an opportunity to constrain dust grain sizes, as the resulting polarized light depends on dust grain size, disk geometry, and optical depth.  

This thesis analyzes multi-wavelength Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) polarization emission observations of the Class I protostellar disk IRS 63 at \bsevenNS, \bsixNS, \bfiveNS, and \bfourNS. The polarization morphology is identified at each wavelength, and revealed a transition from an azimuthal pattern to uniform with the minor axis as wavelength decreases. This shift in pattern indicates a shift in dominant polarization mechanism, with dust self-scattering dominating at shorter wavelengths and in the inner part of the disk. Intensity profiles along the minor axis of the disk indicate that IRS 63 could be geometrically thick and flared.


 Focusing on the central region of the disk and assuming spherical dust grains, our modelling finds the grains are compact with a constrained maximum dust grain size of approximately a few hundred microns. 


Polarization observations in the central region of the disk (where we see dust self-scattering) for polarization fraction is compared to models to constrain dust grain size. The more commonly used shape (simplification) of spherical grains are considered, as well as more accurately but computationally-intensive irregular grain sizes with the help of neural network \texttt{glitterin} from \cite{Li2024}. Spherical grain modelling favours slightly porous grains with a grain size of approximately 245 \micronNS. Models of irregular grains favour a similar maximum grain size of approximately \add{number}. These grain sizes are much larger than the typical interstellar medium dust grains ($\sim$ 0.1 \micronNS) and cloud core grain sizes ($\sim$ 1 \micronNS), providing evidence for dust grain growth in the young disk IRS 63. 

These results highlight how multi-wavelength polarization observations can be used to constrain dust grain size in young protostellar disks. The indication of grain sizes on the order of hundreds of microns in a Class I disk provide evidence that dust grain growth is occurring during this early stage of planet formation, providing some insight into the earlier stages of planet formation.

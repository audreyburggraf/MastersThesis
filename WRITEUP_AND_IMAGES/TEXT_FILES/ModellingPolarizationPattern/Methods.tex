\subsection{Ratio Model}
% ----------------------------------------------------------
The first geometric model we tested is called the \emph{ratio model}. The main idea behind this model is to be able to reproduce the observed polarization morphologies ranging from purely aligned with the minor axis of the disk to purely azimuthal, along with some in-between cases. This was achieved by making a grid of models that are weighted between the two extreme cases. 

The first extreme case is a 100\% uniform pattern where every vector is aligned with the minor axis of the disk. The minor axis of the disk is 90\degg from the disk polarization angle (same as major axis direction, as shown in Figure \ref{fig: DiskAxes}) for each wavelength, found in Table \ref{tab: source_info}. This is shown in the left panel of Figure \ref{fig: RatioExamples}.

The second extreme case is a 100\% azimuthal pattern where the polarization vectors have a circular pattern around the centre of the disk. The centre point of the disk at each wavelength can be found in Table \ref{tab: source_info}. For each pixel $(x, y)$, the offset from the disk centre $(x_c, y_c)$ is calculated using:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \delta x &= x - x_c, \\
    \delta y &= y - y_c,
\end{aligned}
% \label{eq: vectors}
\end{equation}


\noindent which are used to calculate the azimuthal polarization angle using the \texttt{numpy.arctan2} function, which calculates the angle with respect to the disk centre. This is shown in the right panel of Figure \ref{fig: RatioExamples}.



To create the in-between cases, models were generated in steps of 10\%, creating a grid of \SCC{100U -0A}, 90U-10A, ..., 10U-90A, 0\SCC{U}-100A, where U and A refer to the uniform and azimuthal cases, and the numbers are their percentage weights. 

Since the polarization angles cannot be directly combined, the \SQ and \SU for the extreme cases were calculated from the polarization angle grids using the equations:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
Q_{x}
    &= p I \cos(2\theta_x),
     \\
U_{x}
    &=  p I \sin(2\theta_x) ,
\end{aligned}
% \label{eq:50U50A}
\end{equation}

Additionally, $p$ is a constant polarization fraction which was chosen to be 0.02 based on observations presented in \citep{sadavoy_dust_2019} \note{I could be wrong on why we chose this value \SCC{answer}}.



The mock \SQ and $U$ maps from the uniform and azimuthal models were then combined at each chosen ratio. For example, the 50U-50A model was calculated using:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}

    &= 0.5\,Q_{\mathrm{100U-0A}}

U_{\text{50U-50A}}
    &= 0.5\,U_{\mathrm{100U-0A}}
     + 0.5\,U_{\mathrm{0A-100A}},
\end{aligned}
% \label{eq:50U50A}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Gaussian Model}
% ----------------------------------------------------------


The second geometric model we tested is called the \emph{Gaussian model}. The aim of this model was to reproduce a polarization morphology that transitions from being aligned with the minor axis of the disk at the centre to more azimuthal on the outer parts of the disk.
\SC{Give some motivation for this.  We expect the disks to be optically thick at the center more than the edge.}

To achieve this, we used a rotated \SCC{2-dimensional flat-topped Gaussian model} with three free parameters: $\phi$, BMIN and BMAJ, summarized in Table \ref{tab: gaussian_model_info}, and defined below.

\SC{Mention that you had the condition that BMIN <= BMAJ}
The tested values of BMIN and BMAJ control the widths of the Gaussian model. The values are treated as a beam full width half maximum (FWHM), and were converted to standard deviations using the equation:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_i = \frac{\text{FWHM}}{2\sqrt{2 \ln{2}}},
    \label{eq: FWHM_to_sigma}
\end{equation}
\noindent where the subscript of $i$ refers to $m$ or $M$ for minor or major respectively.

The Gaussian was rotated to align with the major axis of the disk (position angle of the disk, Table \ref{tab: source_info}). The coordinates ($x, y$) were shifted with respect to the centre of the disk ($x_c, y_c$) and then rotated:
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix}
X_M \\
Y_m
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\
\sin\theta & \cos\theta
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
x - x_c \\
y - y_c
\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:rotated_coordinates}
\end{equation}



The Gaussian was calculated using the equation:
\begin{equation}
W_{\text{Uniform}} =  I_0 
\exp\left[-\half \left(\left|\frac{X_{M}}{\sigma_M}\right|^\phi + \left|\frac{Y_{m}}{ \sigma_m}\right|^\phi\right)\right],
    \label{eq: gaussian_eq}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $I_0$, the peak value, was calculated using the equation:
\begin{equation}
    I_0 = \frac{1} {\sigma_m \sigma_M \sqrt{2\pi}}.
    \label{eq: Gaussian_model_I0}
\end{equation}

\noindent In Equation \ref{eq: gaussian_eq}, when $\phi=2$ the equation returns a standard 2D Gaussian, and values of $\phi>2$ create a flat-topped Gaussian. The Gaussian was normalized to a maximum of 1 so it could be used as pixel weights. This produced a weighted map of values from 0 to 1, \SCC{where values close to 1 favoured the uniform orientation and values near 0 favoured the azimuthal direction. Conversely, a second map can be created of 1 - Gaussian, where values close to 0 favour azimuthal, and values close to 1 favour uniform orientation}. These two maps act as our weights, rather than a constant like in the ratio model. The weighted \SQ and $U$ were calculated by:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
Q_{\text{Gaussian}}
    &= W_{\text{Uniform}} \,Q_{\mathrm{100U-0A}}
     + (1 - W_{\text{Uniform}})\,Q_{\mathrm{0A-100A}}, \\
U_{\text{Gaussian}}
    &= W_{\text{Uniform}}\,U_{\mathrm{100U-0A}}
     + (1 - W_{\text{Uniform}})\,U_{\mathrm{0A-100A}},
\end{aligned}
% \label{eq:50U50A}
\end{equation}
\SC{change naming here too}


\noindent As in the ratio model, the maps of $Q_{\text{Gaussian}}$ and $U_{\text{Gaussian}}$ were used to calculate the position angle (Equation \ref{eq: PA}).

\subsection{Comparing Geometric Models}
% ----------------------------------------------------------
In both the ratio model and the Gaussian model, many combinations were tested. To quantify the \emph{goodness-of-fit} of each model, a chi-squared value was used. 

First, the difference in the observed and model-predicted polarization angle needed to be calculated carefully. Polarization angles can range from 0-180\degg because polarization measures orientation, but not direction, meaning angles that are 180\degg apart have the same polarization vector orientation. Because of this, two polarization angles that are physically very similar can have a large difference.  For example, the angles of 1\degg and 179\degg only differ by 2\degg, but directly taking the difference of the two would give 178\deggNS. To avoid this issue, the difference is defined by the minimum angular separation between the two angles:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \theta = \text{min}(|\theta_{\text{obs}} - \theta_{\text{model}}|, 180\deggEQ - |\theta_{\text{obs}} - \theta_{\text{model}}|),
    \label{eq: minimum_separation}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\theta_{\text{obs}}$ is the observed polarization angle and $\theta_{\text{model}}$ is the polarization angle from either the ratio or Gaussian model. This angular difference was used in the calculations for the chi-squared value:
\begin{equation}
    \chi^2 = \Sigma (\Delta \theta_i)^2,
    \label{eq: chi_sq}
\end{equation}
\noindent where the angular distance is summed over all sampled polarization vectors. \question{should I be dividing by the polarization angle errors?} \SC{Yes, because you want $\chi^2$ to be unitless.}

The value of $\chi^2$ is then converted to a reduced chi-squared by:
\begin{equation}
    \chi^2_{reduced} = \frac{1}{n-k} \times \chi^2,
    \label{eq: chi_sq_reduced}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $n$ is the number of sampled polarization vectors and $k$ is the degrees of freedom. For the ratio model, there is one degree of freedom, and for the Gaussian method, there are three ($\phi$, BMIN, and BMAJ).
